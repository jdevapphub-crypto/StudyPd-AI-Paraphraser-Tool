How to Paraphrase University Assignments

A practical guide for UNISA, UJ, TUT, and other SA students.

Paraphrasing is one of the most important skills you will learn at university. It is also one of the most commonly misunderstood. Many students think paraphrasing just means "changing a few words", but real paraphrasing is about expressing the same idea in a completely new way while still giving credit to the original author.

This guide walks you through paraphrasing step by step, with examples that work for assignments at UNISA, UJ, TUT, UCT, Wits, and every other South African university.

What paraphrasing actually means

Paraphrasing means restating someone else's idea in your own words while keeping the original meaning. It is different from summarising (which shortens) and different from quoting (which repeats exactly). When you paraphrase, you still cite the source — that is non-negotiable.

Why it matters

Your lecturers want to see that you understand the material, not that you can copy and paste. Paraphrasing shows understanding. It also helps you avoid plagiarism, which is one of the most serious academic offences at any South African university.

Step 1 — Read until you understand

Before you paraphrase, read the original passage twice. Close the source. Then say the idea out loud in your own words. If you cannot say it out loud, you do not understand it well enough to paraphrase it yet.

Step 2 — Change the structure, not just the words

Good paraphrasing changes the sentence structure. If the original sentence starts with the subject, your paraphrase might start with the object. If the original uses the passive voice, yours might use the active voice.

Step 3 — Use your own vocabulary

Swap out words where you can, but do not force it. Some technical terms cannot be changed — "photosynthesis" is still "photosynthesis". What matters is that the phrasing around it is yours.

Step 4 — Cite the source

Every paraphrase must be cited. In APA style, add (Author, Year) at the end of the sentence. In Harvard style, add (Author, Year, p. X). This is how you give credit while still using your own words.

Step 5 — Compare with the original

Put your paraphrase next to the original. If a reader could match them sentence by sentence, you have not paraphrased enough. The structure should be noticeably different.

A quick example

Original: "Students who sleep fewer than six hours per night consistently perform worse on memory-based assessments than those who sleep eight hours."

Weak paraphrase: "Students who sleep less than six hours every night always do worse on memory tests than students who sleep eight hours." (Too close.)

Strong paraphrase: "Research shows a clear link between sleep duration and academic performance: learners getting adequate rest tend to recall information better than those who are chronically sleep-deprived (Smith, 2020)."

Common mistakes

  • Only changing one or two words.
  • Keeping the exact same sentence order.
  • Forgetting to cite the source.
  • Using synonyms that change the meaning.
  • Copying technical terms without quotation marks.

Where StudyPad AI helps

StudyPad AI's paraphraser can show you one possible rewrite of your own writing. Use it to see how sentences can be restructured, then apply those techniques to your source material yourself.

Final thoughts

Paraphrasing is a skill. The more you practice, the faster and better you get. Read widely, write often, and always cite your sources.

Improve Your Academic Writing in South Africa

Practical tips for essays, reports, and assignments.

Academic writing is a skill, not a talent. Every student can improve with deliberate practice. This guide gives you practical, no-nonsense techniques that work for South African students writing in English as a first or second language.

Whether you are writing a UNISA portfolio, a UJ tutorial, a Wits research paper, or a TUT practical report, these principles apply.

1. Know your reader

Your lecturer is your reader. They are busy, they have read hundreds of essays this week, and they want to see that you understand the material. Write for a smart, tired, time-poor reader. That means: clear structure, plain language, no padding.

2. Plan before you write

A ten-minute plan saves an hour of rewriting. Write one sentence for each paragraph you want to include. If you cannot summarise a paragraph in one sentence, you do not yet know what that paragraph is about.

3. One idea per paragraph

Every paragraph should make one point. Start with a topic sentence that states that point. Follow with evidence. End with a short closing sentence that connects back to your main argument.

4. Cut the padding

Phrases like "in today's fast-paced world", "it is important to note", and "in this essay I will discuss" add nothing. Delete them. Say what you mean in the fewest words possible.

5. Use active voice

Passive voice hides who did what. Active voice is clearer and more direct. Compare: "The study was conducted by the researchers" (passive) with "The researchers conducted the study" (active). Prefer active unless the passive is genuinely better.

6. Vary your sentence length

Short sentences make a point. Longer sentences explain and connect. Mix them. Writing that uses only long sentences feels heavy; writing that uses only short sentences feels choppy. The best academic writing moves between both.

7. Cite properly

Every claim that is not common knowledge needs a citation. Use APA, Harvard, MLA, or Chicago — whichever your department requires. Keep a reference list as you go, not at the end when you have forgotten the details.

8. Proofread out loud

Reading aloud catches errors that silent reading misses. You will hear when a sentence is too long, when a word is wrong, and when a paragraph does not flow. Do this before every submission.

9. Read good writing

The best writers are readers. Read academic papers in your field, good journalism, and anything else that is well-written. Over time, good writing patterns will become natural to you.

10. Use tools wisely

Tools like StudyPad AI can help you see alternate phrasings, check the readability of your work, and improve tone. Use them as a support, not as a replacement for your own writing. The goal is to become a better writer, not to hide that you are writing.

Final thoughts

Good academic writing is clear writing. Remove everything that gets in the way of your meaning, and your marks will follow.

Avoid Plagiarism: A Complete Guide for SA Students

Understand it, recognise it, avoid it.

Plagiarism is one of the most serious academic offences at any South African university. It can lead to a failing mark, suspension, or even expulsion. Yet many students commit plagiarism without meaning to — because they do not fully understand what it is. This guide fixes that.

We cover what plagiarism is, the different types, and practical steps you can take to avoid it in every assignment.

What plagiarism actually is

Plagiarism is using someone else's words, ideas, or work as your own without proper acknowledgement. It does not matter whether you did it on purpose or by accident. It is still plagiarism.

The main types

  • Direct plagiarism. Copying a sentence or paragraph word-for-word without quotation marks or citation.
  • Mosaic plagiarism. Mixing copied phrases with your own words without crediting the source.
  • Self-plagiarism. Submitting your own earlier work for a new assignment without permission.
  • Accidental plagiarism. Forgetting to cite, or paraphrasing too closely.
  • Contract cheating. Submitting work written by someone else — a person or an AI — as your own.

Why universities take it so seriously

Academic work is built on trust. When you submit an assignment, you are saying that the work and the thinking are yours. Plagiarism breaks that trust. It also steals from the original author, who gets no credit for their labour.

How to avoid it — practical rules

1. Cite everything that is not common knowledge. Facts, statistics, theories, and opinions all need citations. What counts as "common knowledge" depends on your field — if in doubt, cite it.

2. Use quotation marks for direct quotes. If you use the exact words of a source, put them in quotation marks and add a citation with a page number where required.

3. Paraphrase properly. Change the structure, not just the words. Then cite the source anyway.

4. Keep good notes. As you read sources, note the full reference details immediately — author, year, title, publisher, page. Do not trust your memory.

5. Do not recycle your own work. Unless your lecturer has given permission, do not submit the same essay twice.

6. Do not use AI to write for you. Using AI to generate work you then submit as your own is contract cheating, and most South African universities now treat it that way. Use AI tools only as writing aids, and only where your institution allows it.

What referencing style should you use?

Check your module guide. Most South African universities use APA 7th edition, Harvard, or Chicago. The style matters less than consistency — pick one and use it everywhere.

If you are unsure — ask

If you are unsure whether something needs a citation, ask your lecturer or tutor. Most will appreciate that you took the time to check.

Final thoughts

Plagiarism is easy to avoid once you know the rules. Cite everything, paraphrase properly, and never submit work that is not your own. Your academic reputation is worth more than any single assignment.

NSFAS Motivation Letter Tips That Actually Work

Write a letter that shows real need and real drive.

The NSFAS motivation letter is one of the most important pieces of writing any South African student will do. It is your chance to explain, in your own words, why you deserve funding and what you plan to do with your education. This guide shows you how to write one that actually works.

NSFAS receives hundreds of thousands of applications. A well-written motivation letter does not guarantee funding, but a poorly written one can lose you a place. Take it seriously.

What NSFAS is actually looking for

NSFAS wants to fund students who (1) genuinely cannot afford tertiary study, and (2) are likely to succeed and use their qualification to contribute to South Africa. Your letter should make both points clearly and honestly.

Structure that works

  • Opening. State who you are, what you are studying (or plan to study), and that you are applying for NSFAS funding.
  • Financial situation. Explain your household's income honestly. Mention who supports you, how many dependants there are, and any specific hardship.
  • Academic record. Show that you have worked hard and that funding will not be wasted on you.
  • Future plans. Explain what you want to do after your qualification and how it will help your community or the country.
  • Closing. Thank the reader and confirm that all information is true.

Tip 1 — Be honest

Do not exaggerate your hardship. NSFAS verifies information, and false statements can disqualify you permanently. Say what is true, in plain language.

Tip 2 — Be specific

"I am poor" is weak. "My mother is the only breadwinner, earning R3,200 a month, supporting four children" is strong. Numbers and details matter.

Tip 3 — Keep it short

One page is enough. NSFAS reviewers read thousands of letters. A clear, well-organised one-page letter beats a rambling two-page letter every time.

Tip 4 — Use plain English

You do not need big words. Write the way you speak — clear, simple, direct. If a sentence feels difficult to read, rewrite it shorter.

Tip 5 — Show drive, not just need

Many applicants are poor. Fewer are poor and have a clear plan. Talk about what you will do with the qualification. Do you want to be a teacher in your home village? A nurse in a rural clinic? A civil engineer who builds roads in the Eastern Cape? Make it concrete.

Tip 6 — Proofread properly

Read your letter out loud. Fix typos. Ask a teacher, parent, or friend to read it. A letter with errors suggests carelessness, which is the opposite of what you want.

Tip 7 — Use StudyPad AI as a writing aid

You can paste your draft into StudyPad AI to check readability and improve the tone. Do not let the tool write the letter for you — the voice must be yours. Use it only to make your own writing clearer.

Final thoughts

A NSFAS motivation letter is a chance to tell your story. Be honest, be specific, be brief, and be clear. If you do those four things, you have given yourself the best possible chance.
